## Main terms

**Process and discrete plants**: Process plants transform material and count in tonnes or batches (plastics, chemicals, food). Discrete plants build things you can count (cars, machines). Polymex compounds plastics: a process plant.

**Lean**: The operating system born in post-war Japan that attacks three brakes on value added: rigidity, waste and variability. Value added is what the customer is willing to pay for; everything else is a brake.

**The eight wastes**: Overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, rework and defects, unnecessary motion, and unused skills and ideas. For example, a batch larger than ordered, run to avoid a changeover.

**The 5M**: The five sources of variability: materials, means, methods, milieu and messages. A resin lot that melts differently is materials; a plan nobody can run is messages.

**The lean house**: Best customer service as the roof, two pillars (just-in-time: make only what is needed, when it is needed; jidoka: stop at the first defect), on three foundations: stable standards, shop-floor performance management and problem solving.

**Lean maturity**: How far a plant has gone, from firefighting to standards, daily management and an improvement culture. It is the fastest read of a plant, and it tells you how reliable its data is and how fast people will adopt a new tool.

**Contribution margin**: Revenue minus variable costs: what one more unit is worth. One tonne is worth its contribution margin, never its price or its full cost.

**The demand fork**: The question that decides what a freed hour is worth: can the extra output be sold? If yes, it is worth the contribution margin. If not, only the variable cost it avoids, such as overtime or energy.

**Count every euro once**: Overtime is usually a symptom of changeovers, and inventory releases cash once: never add a symptom to its cause, or the same euro twice.

**The five filters**: How to choose where to start: does the gain reach the P&L, is it at least ten times what it costs, is the plant ready, is there a sponsor, and will the plant exist at this scale in three years?

**Knowledge bundle**: The engagement's single source of truth, one folder in the Open Knowledge Format, as on Day 1. Today's bundle holds everything the diagnostic team collected on the Polymex group.

**Six-phase method**: The shape of every FDE engagement: embed, find, build, deploy, drive adoption, expand. Each phase ends on an exit criterion the sponsor can see.

**Project charter and kill criteria**: The charter turns your choice into a contract: target and date, scope in and out, named roles, cost and governance. Kill criteria are measurable conditions that stop the project, written on day one.

**No-surprises rule**: Bad news reaches the sponsor the same day, with two or three options and your recommendation.

**Baseline and validated gain**: The baseline is the agreed reference a gain is measured against, settled with the controller in week one. A gain is validated when the client's controller computes it without you and finance signs it.

## The ideas to hold on to

- Know which game a plant is playing before you judge it: process or discrete, flow or project, made to stock or to order.
- Lean taught plants to see waste; AI lets them act on all of it, every shift. AI amplifies the operating system, it does not replace it.
- A gain that never reaches the P&L is a story, not a gain: a freed hour in a half-empty plant can be worth zero euros.
- In a group, start from the group's P&L and strategy, not from the loudest plant: the worst KPI is often not the place to start.
- The gain is announced by the controller's spreadsheet, not yours, from a protocol signed in week one.
