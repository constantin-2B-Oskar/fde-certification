## Claude Code is an agent in your terminal: it reads, plans, edits and runs, and asks before it acts

1. **Reads.** The files in your repository, starting with `CLAUDE.md`.
2. **Plans.** Proposes the steps before touching anything, when you ask it to.
3. **Edits.** Changes files and shows you the diff.
4. **Runs.** Executes commands and tests, and reads the output.
5. **Asks.** For permission before editing or running, unless you allowed it.

<!-- 30 | Detail -->
## Keep control: review every diff, approve every command, never approve what you cannot read

- Start with plan mode for anything larger than a small fix: read the plan, then let it build.
- Approve commands one by one at first. Allow a command permanently only once you trust it.
- Never approve a command that deletes, pushes or deploys without reading it.
- If the session drifts, stop it, restate the goal and start a fresh session.

## Why this job exists

<!-- 41 | Detail -->
## Operations run on thousands of small decisions a day, and that is where AI pays

- Which order runs next on line 3. Whether to release batch 2241. How many bags of pigment to reorder.
- Which recipe meets the new customer specification. Which technician goes to which site first.
- Each decision is small; together they set throughput, cost, cash and service.
- Today most of them are taken in spreadsheets and heads, with yesterday's data.

---
<!-- 42 | Data -->
## Most AI pilots never reach the P&L, and the cause is rarely the model

- About 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots showed no measurable P&L impact.
- Projects run with specialized partners succeeded about twice as often as internal builds.
- The study traces the gap to how tools are integrated into work and whether they adapt, not to model quality.

Source: MIT NANDA, The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025, July 2025. Failure is defined as no measurable benefit within six months.

<!-- 43 | Detail -->
## The model is the same for every plant; the plant is not

- Every competitor can rent the same models, at falling prices.
- No two plants share the same machines, products, recipes, rules and people.
- The value is in fitting the model to one operation: its data, its rules, its decisions.
- That fitting is done on site, by a person. That person is the FDE.

---
<!-- 44 | Detail -->
## Agents can now carry an operational task end to end, with a person approving

| Setting | A task an agent can carry |
|---|---|
| Plant | Read the new orders, check them against the line rules, propose a slot, flag the risk to the planner |
| Warehouse | Rebuild tomorrow's waves when a truck is late, and tell the shift leader why |
| Field service | Re-route three technicians around an urgent job, and order the part for the van |
| Utility network | Turn an inspection report into a ranked list of work orders |

In every case, a person approves before anything changes in a system or on a machine.

---
<!-- 45 | Detail -->
## We have seen this gap before: electricity paid off only when factories were redesigned around it

- Electric motors arrived in factories in the 1880s; the productivity surge came in the 1920s.
- The gain did not come from swapping the steam engine for a big motor, but from redesigning the plant around many small ones.
- The same holds now: the gain comes from redesigning the work around the model, one workflow at a time.
- That redesign, inside a client's operation, is the FDE's job.

Source: Paul A. David, The Dynamo and the Computer, American Economic Review, 1990.

## Industrial AI fails in the last mile: data, workflow and people

| Layer | What goes wrong |
|---|---|
| Model | Rarely the problem anymore |
| Data | Lives in legacy systems, spreadsheets and heads; worse than anyone expected |
| Workflow | The tool is live, but decisions still run on habit |
| People | Operators override what they do not trust, and they are often right |

The FDE is the person who closes the last three rows.

## In industrial operations the job is harder: continuous shifts, old systems, safety and thin margins

- A plant runs day and night; a bad schedule costs tonnes by the next shift.
- Systems are decades old; the rules that matter sit in spreadsheets and notebooks.
- Safety comes first and slows everything you do on the floor.
- In field service and utilities, the floor is spread over hundreds of kilometres: a substation, a customer site, a van.
- Margins are thin: a plant at 6.6% EBIT cannot afford a pilot that changes nothing.

---
<!-- 52 | Detail -->
## European industry needs it now: experts are retiring, and the rules leave with them

- The planners and operators who know the plant's real rules are often in the last decade of their careers.
- Their rules are rarely written down; when they leave, the plant relearns them through scrap and late orders.
- Capturing those rules in a system the next generation can use is one of the highest-value things an FDE does.
- You will meet one such expert on Thursday.

- Classic IT services sell time and deliverables; the client carries the risk that nothing changes.

## Responsibilities: from the first interview to the gain in the controller's spreadsheet

1. Embed with the plant team; map the process, the systems and the people.
2. Find and size the problem worth solving, with the sponsor and the controller.
3. Capture the data and the experts' rules, and turn them into a model of the plant.
4. Build, integrate and deploy the system on the client's real data and workflows.
5. Drive adoption until decisions run on the system without you.
6. Prove the gain with the client's finance team, and feed field patterns back to the product.

## What you are measured on

| Measure | What it means |
|---|---|
| Gain signed by finance | Euros a year, computed by the client's controller from a protocol agreed in week one |
| Time to first value | Weeks from arrival to a decision taken with the system |
| Adoption | Named users who rely on the system every week |
| Expansion | The next problem or the next site, decided by the client |
| Product feedback | Field patterns turned into product changes |

Not on hours billed, lines of code or slides produced: none of these appears in the client's P&L.

## The FDE loop: embed, find, build, deploy, drive adoption, expand

1. **Embed** with the client team, on site.
2. **Find** the problem worth solving, in euros.

## Planning and scheduling: turn the planner's rules into a plan the floor can run

|---|---|

## Quality: find the defect where it is made, not where it is found

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Decision | Release or hold a batch; where to inspect; which root cause to fix first |
| Data | Lab results, in-line measurements, defect logs with photos, process parameters, raw-material lots and certificates |
| Systems | LIMS or quality module, MES, historian, ERP for lots and traceability |
| People | Quality manager (decides), lab technicians, operators who log defects, the customer's quality team |
| Gain | Scrap and rework cost, inspection effort put where it matters, complaints and claims avoided |
| Trap | Defects are logged where they are found; nobody measures how long they took to find |

## In quality, when a defect is found matters as much as how many

- A defect caught at the machine has cost a fraction of one caught at final inspection, where all the cost is already spent.
- So the first number to build is often detection latency: the time between cause and detection.
- Root-cause analysis is slow when lab, machine, material and maintenance data sit in separate systems. Joining them is FDE work, before any model.
- At Polymex, scrap is 4.2% of €89M of material input. You will price it tomorrow morning.

## Supply chain and inventory: set how much to hold, of what, and where, every week

## Engineering and recipes: capture why the experts rule out what they never try

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Decision | Which ingredients and proportions, which process settings, to meet a specification at a cost |
| Data | Past formulations and their measured properties, lab tests, raw-material specifications, process parameters, the chemists' rules |

| People | Formulation chemist or process engineer (the rules), R&D lead (decides), lab (tests), production (can it run?) |
| Gain | Fewer trial iterations, faster time to specification, cheaper or more recycled material at the same performance |
| Trap | The data holds only the trials people chose to run; the rules that excluded the rest are in their heads |

## OEE multiplies three ratios, each computed from sums

| Ratio | Formula |
|---|---|
| Availability | Run minutes ÷ planned minutes |
| Performance | Output ÷ (run hours × ideal rate) |
| Quality | (Output − scrap) ÷ output |
| OEE | Availability × performance × quality |

Sum minutes and tonnes over the period first, then divide. Never average per-row ratios.

## The constraint is the stage that is always busy and never waits

- Upstream of the constraint, stages are blocked: they cannot hand over their work.
- Downstream, stages are starved: they wait for work.
- The constraint itself is neither starved nor blocked for long.
- `stage_daily.csv` gives you the minutes. Show the evidence, not only the answer.
