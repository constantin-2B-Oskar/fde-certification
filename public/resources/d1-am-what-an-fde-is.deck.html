Why this job exists
Operations run on thousands of small decisions a day, and that is where AI pays
Which order runs next on line 3. Whether to release batch 2241. How many bags of pigment to reorder.
Which recipe meets the new customer specification. Which technician goes to which site first.
Each decision is small; together they set throughput, cost, cash and service.
Today most of them are taken in spreadsheets and heads, with yesterday's data.

About 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots showed no measurable P&L impact.
Projects run with specialized partners succeeded about twice as often as internal builds.

Every competitor can rent the same models, at falling prices.
No two plants share the same machines, products, recipes, rules and people.
The value is in fitting the model to one operation: its data, its rules, its decisions.
That fitting is done on site, by a person. That person is the FDE.

FDE School · Day 1 · Morning44 / 122
Why this job exists
We have seen this gap before: electricity paid off only when factories were redesigned around it
Electric motors arrived in factories in the 1880s; the productivity surge came in the 1920s.
The gain did not come from swapping the steam engine for a big motor, but from redesigning the plant around many small ones.
The same holds now: the gain comes from redesigning the work around the model, one workflow at a time.
That redesign, inside a client's operation, is the FDE's job.
Source: Paul A. David, The Dynamo and the Computer, American Economic Review, 1990.

FDE School · Day 1 · Morning50 / 122
Why this job exists
In industrial operations the job is harder: continuous shifts, old systems, safety and thin margins
A plant runs day and night; a bad schedule costs tonnes by the next shift.
Systems are decades old; the rules that matter sit in spreadsheets and notebooks.
Safety comes first and slows everything you do on the floor.
In field service and utilities, the floor is spread over hundreds of kilometres: a substation, a customer site, a van.
Margins are thin: a plant at 6.6% EBIT cannot afford a pilot that changes nothing.

OEE multiplies three ratios, each computed from sums
Ratio
Formula
Availability
Run minutes ÷ planned minutes
Performance
Output ÷ (run hours × ideal rate)
Quality
(Output − scrap) ÷ output
OEE
Availability × performance × quality

Sum minutes and tonnes over the period first, then divide. Never average per-row ratios.
